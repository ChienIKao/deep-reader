Critical Reading Notes

On the Duty of Civil Disobedience Henry David Thoreau · 1849 · Opening excerpt, ¶1–¶7

In one sentence: Because government is only a tool and majority rule rests on force rather than justice, a person must follow conscience before law, and refuse to be associated with a government that protects slavery and wages an unjust war.

1Argument What is the author arguing?

Thoreau argues that a citizen’s first duty is to his own conscience, not to the State. Government has no authority of its own; it is only an expedient, a tool the people use to carry out their will, and it is easily hijacked by a few. Majority rule cannot fix this, because the majority rules by strength, not by being right. So when the American government protects slavery and fights the Mexican War, a decent person cannot cooperate with it without disgrace. His position is radical in principle but moderate in practice: he asks “not at once no government, but at once a better government.”

2Argumentation How is the argument built, step by step?

  1. Start from the reader’s own belief. He accepts the popular motto “that government is best which governs least” and pushes it to its logical end: none at all. ¶1
  2. Lower the status of government. Analogy (standing army → standing government), a current example (the Mexican War), and satire show government as a tool that is often in the way. ¶1–2
  3. Block the obvious objection. He is not an anarchist; he wants a better government now. ¶3
  4. Attack the source of authority. By elimination, majority rule rests on force, so it cannot decide right and wrong; conscience must. ¶4
  5. Show the cost of obedience. Soldiers marching against their conscience become “machines.” Service by body, by head, and by conscience are ranked, and only the last is truly moral. ¶4–6
  6. Apply the principle to today. A government that is also “the slave’s government” cannot be recognized as one’s own. ¶7

3Evidence What textual evidence supports it?

  • Government is at best but an expedient ¶1 — the basic definition that the rest of the essay depends on.
  • the work of comparatively a few individuals using the standing government as their tool ¶1 — a real event his readers knew, showing how the tool gets abused.
  • because they are physically the strongest ¶4 — the key step that separates majority rule from justice.
  • not as men mainly, but as machines, with their bodies ¶5 — the image that turns “good citizens” into a problem.
  • he cannot without disgrace be associated with it ¶7 — the practical conclusion.

4Structure What role does each part play?

  • ¶1–2Setup. Redefines government as a tool and weakens its prestige, so the reader is ready to question obedience.
  • ¶3Hinge. Moves from ideal to practice and protects the author from the charge of anarchism.
  • ¶4Core. The theoretical heart: conscience above majority and law.
  • ¶5–6Redefinition. Reclassifies citizens and flips what “good citizenship” means.
  • ¶7Application. Turns the principle into a concrete demand about the present government.

5Key Insights What is worth remembering?

Concepts

  • expedient: government as a means, never an end.
  • the right vs. the law: legality is not justice.
  • men vs. subjects: moral agency comes first.

Analytical moves

  • Reductio: takes the reader’s premise to its extreme.
  • Elimination: rules out every basis of majority authority except force.
  • Reversal: the most sincere service looks like betrayal.

Questions to discuss

  • If conscience outranks the majority, whose conscience counts?
  • Does he ignore the fairness of democratic procedure?
  • Is withdrawal more effective than reform from within?

On the Duty of Civil Disobedience

論公民不服從的義務（開篇節選）

Henry David Thoreau · 1849 · Source: Project Gutenberg (public domain) · Opening excerpt, ¶1–¶7

In one sentence: Starting from the motto "that government is best which governs least," Thoreau argues that when a government protects slavery and wages an unjust war, people must follow their conscience rather than the law or the majority — "men first, and subjects afterward."

English OriginalChinese Translation

I heartily accept the motto,—“That government is best which governs least;” and I should like to see it acted up to more rapidly and systematically. Carried out, it finally amounts to this, which also I believe—“That government is best which governs not at all;” and when men are prepared for it, that will be the kind of government which they will have. Government is at best but an expedient; but most governments are usually, and all governments are sometimes, inexpedient. The objections which have been brought against a standing army, and they are many and weighty, and deserve to prevail, may also at last be brought against a standing government. The standing army is only an arm of the standing government. The government itself, which is only the mode which the people have chosen to execute their will, is equally liable to be abused and perverted before the people can act through it. Witness the present Mexican war, the work of comparatively a few individuals using the standing government as their tool; for, in the outset, the people would not have consented to this measure.

我衷心接受這句格言：「管得最少的政府，就是最好的政府」；我也希望看到它被更迅速、更有系統地付諸實行。這句話若貫徹到底，最終就等於這一句——而這一句我同樣相信——「完全不管的政府，才是最好的政府」；等到人們準備好了，那就會是他們將擁有的政府。政府充其量不過是一種權宜之計（expedient）；然而大多數政府通常、而所有政府有時，都是不得其宜的（inexpedient）。〔譯注：expedient／inexpedient 是文字遊戲——政府本應是「方便行事的工具」，卻往往成了「礙事」的東西。〕人們對常備軍提出的種種反對意見——這些意見既多又有分量，理應佔上風——最終也同樣可以用來反對一個常設的政府。常備軍不過是常設政府的一條手臂。政府本身，只是人民選來執行其意志的一種方式，但在人民還來不及透過它行動之前，它同樣容易遭到濫用和扭曲。看看眼前的墨西哥戰爭吧：那是相對少數的人把常設政府當作工具所幹下的事；因為一開始，人民根本不會同意這項舉措。

This American government,—what is it but a tradition, though a recent one, endeavoring to transmit itself unimpaired to posterity, but each instant losing some of its integrity? It has not the vitality and force of a single living man; for a single man can bend it to his will. It is a sort of wooden gun to the people themselves; and, if ever they should use it in earnest as a real one against each other, it will surely split. But it is not the less necessary for this; for the people must have some complicated machinery or other, and hear its din, to satisfy that idea of government which they have. Governments show thus how successfully men can be imposed on, even impose on themselves, for their own advantage. It is excellent, we must all allow; yet this government never of itself furthered any enterprise, but by the alacrity with which it got out of its way. It does not keep the country free. It does not settle the West. It does not educate. The character inherent in the American people has done all that has been accomplished; and it would have done somewhat more, if the government had not sometimes got in its way. For government is an expedient, by which men would fain succeed in letting one another alone; and, as has been said, when it is most expedient, the governed are most let alone by it. Trade and commerce, if they were not made of India rubber, would never manage to bounce over obstacles which legislators are continually putting in their way; and, if one were to judge these men wholly by the effects of their actions, and not partly by their intentions, they would deserve to be classed and punished with those mischievous persons who put obstructions on the railroads.

這個美國政府——它不就是一種傳統嗎？雖然是個新近的傳統，竭力想完好無損地傳給後代，卻每時每刻都在喪失它的一部分完整。它沒有一個活人所具有的生命力與力量；因為單單一個人就能讓它屈從於自己的意志。對人民自己來說，它就像一把木頭做的槍；萬一他們真把它當成真槍來互相攻擊，它一定會裂開。但它並不因此就比較不必要；因為人民總得有某種複雜的機器，聽聽它的轟鳴，才能滿足他們心中對「政府」的那種想像。政府由此顯示：人可以多麼成功地被擺布，甚至為了自身的好處而自己擺布自己。我們都得承認，這很了不起；然而這個政府從不曾憑自身推動過任何事業——除非是靠它讓開路時的那份爽快。它沒有讓這個國家保持自由。它沒有開拓西部。它沒有從事教育。美國人民與生俱來的品格，成就了已經完成的一切；而要不是政府有時擋了路，這份品格還會成就更多。因為政府是一種權宜之計，人們藉由它，希望能成功地讓彼此互不干涉；而且如前所述，當政府最「得宜」的時候，被統治者也就最不受它干涉。商業與貿易若不是橡膠做的，絕不可能彈跳越過立法者不斷在它們路上設下的障礙；而如果完全根據這些人行為的後果、而不是部分根據他們的意圖來評判，他們就活該被歸入那些在鐵軌上放置障礙物的搗蛋分子之列，並受到同樣的懲罰。

But, to speak practically and as a citizen, unlike those who call themselves no-government men, I ask for, not at once no government, but at once a better government. Let every man make known what kind of government would command his respect, and that will be one step toward obtaining it.

但是，務實地、以一個公民的身分來說，我不同於那些自稱「無政府派」（no-government men）的人：我要求的不是立刻廢除政府，而是立刻有一個更好的政府。讓每個人都表明，什麼樣的政府能贏得他的尊敬，這就是朝著得到這樣的政府邁出的一步。

After all, the practical reason why, when the power is once in the hands of the people, a majority are permitted, and for a long period continue, to rule, is not because they are most likely to be in the right, nor because this seems fairest to the minority, but because they are physically the strongest. But a government in which the majority rule in all cases can not be based on justice, even as far as men understand it. Can there not be a government in which the majorities do not virtually decide right and wrong, but conscience?—in which majorities decide only those questions to which the rule of expediency is applicable? Must the citizen ever for a moment, or in the least degree, resign his conscience to the legislator? Why has every man a conscience, then? I think that we should be men first, and subjects afterward. It is not desirable to cultivate a respect for the law, so much as for the right. The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right. It is truly enough said that a corporation has no conscience; but a corporation of conscientious men is a corporation with a conscience. Law never made men a whit more just; and, by means of their respect for it, even the well-disposed are daily made the agents of injustice. A common and natural result of an undue respect for the law is, that you may see a file of soldiers, colonel, captain, corporal, privates, powder-monkeys and all, marching in admirable order over hill and dale to the wars, against their wills, aye, against their common sense and consciences, which makes it very steep marching indeed, and produces a palpitation of the heart. They have no doubt that it is a damnable business in which they are concerned; they are all peaceably inclined. Now, what are they? Men at all? or small movable forts and magazines, at the service of some unscrupulous man in power? Visit the Navy Yard, and behold a marine, such a man as an American government can make, or such as it can make a man with its black arts, a mere shadow and reminiscence of humanity, a man laid out alive and standing, and already, as one may say, buried under arms with funeral accompaniment, though it may be

說到底，一旦權力落入人民手中，多數人之所以被允許統治、而且長期持續統治，實際的理由既不是因為他們最可能是對的，也不是因為這對少數人似乎最公平，而是因為他們在體力上最強。但一個在所有情況下都由多數統治的政府，不可能建立在正義之上——即使是人們所理解的那種正義也不行。難道不能有這樣一個政府：實際上決定是非對錯的不是多數，而是良知？——在這樣的政府裡，多數只決定那些適用「權宜法則」的問題？公民難道必須在任何時刻、以任何程度，把自己的良知交給立法者嗎？那麼，人為什麼還要有良知？我認為，我們應該先是人，然後才是臣民。與其培養對法律的尊重，不如培養對「正當」（the right）的尊重。我有權承擔的唯一義務，就是在任何時候都去做我認為正確的事。人們說得很對：法人團體沒有良知；但由有良知的人組成的團體，就是一個有良知的團體。法律從未讓人變得更公正一絲一毫；反而正因為人們尊重法律，連心地善良的人也每天都在淪為不義的執行者。過度尊重法律的一個常見而自然的結果，就是你會看到一列士兵——上校、上尉、下士、列兵、搬火藥的童兵等等——以令人讚嘆的整齊隊形翻山越谷開赴戰場，違背他們的意志，是的，違背他們的常識與良知，這使得行軍格外艱難，令人心悸。他們毫不懷疑自己參與的是一樁該死的勾當；他們全都愛好和平。那麼，他們是什麼？還算是人嗎？還是為某個無恥的當權者效勞的小型移動堡壘與彈藥庫？去海軍造船廠看看吧，看看一名海軍陸戰隊員——那就是美國政府所能造就的人，或者說，它用它的妖術所能把人變成的樣子——只是人性的一道影子、一段殘存的記憶，一個被活生生停屍、站著的人，可以說已經在送葬的樂聲中被埋葬在武器之下，雖然情形或許是

The mass of men serve the State thus, not as men mainly, but as machines, with their bodies. They are the standing army, and the militia, jailers, constables, posse comitatus, &c. In most cases there is no free exercise whatever of the judgment or of the moral sense; but they put themselves on a level with wood and earth and stones; and wooden men can perhaps be manufactured that will serve the purpose as well. Such command no more respect than men of straw, or a lump of dirt. They have the same sort of worth only as horses and dogs. Yet such as these even are commonly esteemed good citizens. Others, as most legislators, politicians, lawyers, ministers, and office-holders, serve the state chiefly with their heads; and, as they rarely make any moral distinctions, they are as likely to serve the devil, without intending it, as God. A very few, as heroes, patriots, martyrs, reformers in the great sense, and men, serve the State with their consciences also, and so necessarily resist it for the most part; and they are commonly treated by it as enemies. A wise man will only be useful as a man, and will not submit to be “clay,” and “stop a hole to keep the wind away,” but leave that office to his dust at least:

¶1

I heartily accept the motto,—“That government is best which governs least;” and I should like to see it acted up to more rapidly and systematically. Carried out, it finally amounts to this, which also I believe—“That government is best which governs not at all;” and when men are prepared for it, that will be the kind of government which they will have. Government is at best but an expedient; but most governments are usually, and all governments are sometimes, inexpedient. The objections which have been brought against a standing army, and they are many and weighty, and deserve to prevail, may also at last be brought against a standing government. The standing army is only an arm of the standing government. The government itself, which is only the mode which the people have chosen to execute their will, is equally liable to be abused and perverted before the people can act through it. Witness the present Mexican war, the work of comparatively a few individuals using the standing government as their tool; for, in the outset, the people would not have consented to this measure.

Q1 Role of Government
Function
Opening claim. Thoreau borrows a popular motto, pushes it to its logical end, and sets the essay's basic view of government: it is only an expedient (a tool for getting something done).
Reasoning
“Governs least is best” → taken to its limit → “governs not at all is best,” but only when men are prepared for it. He then shows how government gets abused with an analogy (the problems of a standing army are the problems of a standing government) and a current example (a few people used the government to start the Mexican War).
Answers
Q1 · Claim: Government has no value of its own. It is just “the mode which the people have chosen to execute their will,” and it fails that role once a small group takes control of it.
¶2

This American government,—what is it but a tradition, though a recent one, endeavoring to transmit itself unimpaired to posterity, but each instant losing some of its integrity? It has not the vitality and force of a single living man; for a single man can bend it to his will. Q1 Role of Government
Function
Evidence and satire. A chain of images (a wooden gun, noisy machinery, India rubber) strips government of its dignity.
Reasoning
He concedes first (“it is not the less necessary,” “It is excellent”), then turns with yet: government's only achievement is getting out of the way. Three short sentences (“It does not…”) give all national success to the people's character, and lawmakers are compared to people who block railroads.
Answers
Q1 · Evidence: The list supports “government is a tool, even an obstacle.” Note that rhetoric outweighs argument here: “never of itself furthered any enterprise” is a sweeping overstatement, useful material for Q1's limitations.
¶3

Q1

What is Thoreau’s view of the proper role and limits of government?

Core Argument

Thoreau treats government as a pure tool (an expedient). Its only value is carrying out the people’s will and letting people “let one another alone”; it has no authority of its own. Ideally, the less government the better, and finally none at all, but only once “men are prepared for it.” His view is best described as anarchist in the ideal, reformist in practice.

Key Evidence

  • Government is at best but an expedient ¶1 — the essay’s basic definition: government has only instrumental value.
  • a few individuals using the standing government as their tool ¶1 — the Mexican War shows how easily a small group can hijack the tool.
  • It does not keep the country free. It does not settle the West. It does not educate. ¶2 — credits the nation’s achievements to the people, denying government any positive role.

Counterpoints & Limits

  • Overgeneralization: ¶2’s claim that government “never of itself furthered any enterprise” ignores public works, the postal service, and the legal order that protects trade, all of which Thoreau himself benefited from.
  • Internal tension: ¶1 longs for a government that “governs not at all,” while ¶3 asks only for “a better government.” The phrase “when men are prepared for it” bridges the gap, but he never says what being prepared means.

Q2

Why does Thoreau reject majority rule as a basis for justice?

Core Argument

By elimination, Thoreau argues that the majority rules not because it is more likely to be right, nor because it is fairer, but because it is physically strongest. Authority that comes from force has nothing to do with right and wrong, so a government in which the majority decides “in all cases” cannot rest on justice. He does not reject majority rule entirely; he limits it. Majorities may settle questions of expediency, but moral questions belong to conscience.

Key Evidence

  • because they are physically the strongest ¶4 — reveals force as the real basis of majority rule.
  • majorities decide only those questions to which the rule of expediency is applicable ¶4 — draws the boundary where majority rule is legitimate.
  • Law never made men a whit more just ¶4 — laws made by the majority do not create justice on their own.

Counterpoints & Limits

  • Ignores procedural legitimacy: Democratic theory holds that majority rule is legitimate because it treats each person’s judgment equally and settles disagreement peacefully, not just because of numbers. Thoreau may be attacking a weak version of the opposing view.
  • Whose conscience?: In Thoreau’s time, some people defended slavery “by conscience.” If conscience outranks the majority, whose conscience counts? Thoreau gives no test.
  • Blurry line: Who decides which questions are about expediency and which are about morality?

Q3

How does Thoreau classify the ways people serve the State, and what does this imply about good citizenship?

Core Argument

Thoreau ranks service to the State in three levels: with the body (soldiers, jailers: like machines), with the head (legislators, lawyers: no moral distinctions), and with the conscience (heroes, reformers: necessarily resist the State). This turns common sense upside down. The “good citizens” society praises are merely obedient bodies, while those who serve by conscience are treated as enemies. The core of good citizenship is not obedience but keeping one’s moral judgment: “men first, and subjects afterward.”

Key Evidence

  • we should be men first, and subjects afterward ¶4 — sets the order of identities.
  • not as men mainly, but as machines, with their bodies ¶5 — the lowest tier of the classification.
  • serve the State with their consciences also, and so necessarily resist it ¶5 — the highest form of service shows up as resistance.
  • appears to them useless and selfish ¶6 — society’s judgment is reversed.

Counterpoints & Limits

  • Elitism: Only “a very few” reach the level of conscience, and ordinary soldiers are compared to “horses and dogs.” This is harsh toward people with little choice, such as conscripts.
  • Too simple a split: Most people take part in public life with body, head, and conscience at once. “Obedience = giving up morality” does not always hold: some people obey the law because of their conscience.

Q4

What does Thoreau ask the individual to do, and how radical is his position?

Core Argument

Within this excerpt, Thoreau asks two things. First, speak up: say what kind of government would earn your respect (¶3). Second, disassociate: refuse to recognize a slave-holding government as “my government” (¶7). His position is radical in principle (conscience outranks law and the majority) but restrained in method (no immediate abolition of government, no call for violence). What is radical is that he cancels the duty to obey, not that he wants to overthrow the government.

Key Evidence

  • not at once no government, but at once a better government ¶3 — explicitly rejects the anarchist label.
  • The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right ¶4 — the principle behind action.
  • he cannot without disgrace be associated with it ¶7 — the concrete conclusion: cut ties with an unjust government.

Counterpoints & Limits

  • Method unclear: What does “not being associated” with the government actually involve? The excerpt does not say (refusing to pay taxes comes later).
  • Does it work?: Can one person’s withdrawal change an institution? Would reform from inside, through voting and legislation, be more effective?
  • Privilege: Cutting ties assumes you can bear the consequences. For enslaved people and the powerless, this option does not exist.

Argument Breakdown (Toulmin Model)

Argument 1: Majority rule cannot be the basis of justice

Claim

A government in which the majority rules in all cases cannot be based on justice.

Grounds

The majority rules because it is physically strongest, not because it is most likely right or fairest. (¶4)

Warrant

(implicit) If a way of deciding draws its authority from force, it cannot guarantee that its results are just.

Backing

Everyone has a conscience — “Why has every man a conscience, then?” — so moral judgment belongs to the individual and cannot be handed over.

Qualifier

“In all cases”: the majority may still decide questions “to which the rule of expediency is applicable.”

Rebuttal

If majority rule is legitimate because of fair procedure and equal respect for each person’s judgment, not because of force, the grounds of this argument are weakened.

Claim

A person cannot be associated with the present American government without disgrace. (¶7)

Grounds

“Today”: aimed at this particular government now, not at every government.

Reform from inside the system (voting, legislation) may work better than withdrawing. Not answered in this excerpt; later in the essay Thoreau discusses the limits of voting.

Evaluating the Argument

Hidden Assumptions

  • Conscience is a reliable moral guide: the most important and the weakest. Consciences differ, and in Thoreau’s time some people defended slavery by conscience. He does not say how to settle conflicts between consciences.
  • Government has only tool value: debatable. Social-contract theorists argue that government does more than carry out the people’s will; it also creates and protects rights.
  • Taking part means sharing the guilt: fairly strong, but it comes in degrees. Do paying taxes, voting, and serving in the army carry the same moral responsibility?

Gaps & Fallacy Risks

  • Overgeneralization (¶2): “this government never of itself furthered any enterprise” is a rhetorical sweeping claim that ignores public works, the postal service, and the legal order.
  • Straw man risk (¶4): reduces the case for majority rule to “force” and skips the defense based on fair procedure.
  • Appeal to emotion (¶4–5): comparing soldiers to wood, horses, and dogs is powerful rhetoric, but contempt stands in for argument.

Strongest Objection & the Author’s Likely Reply

Objection (Hobbesian / democratic-procedure view): In a society where people disagree about morality, if everyone treats their own conscience as the highest authority, law can no longer coordinate behavior, and society falls back into everyone doing as they please. The value of majority rule is that it settles disagreement peacefully and treats each person’s judgment equally.

Thoreau’s likely reply: He only asks conscience to come first on major moral questions (slavery, wars of conquest); everyday questions of “expediency” can still go to the majority. And his method is nonviolent: he accepts the consequences himself and does not force anyone else.

Assessment: The reply partly works. Nonviolence and accepting the consequences lower the risk of disorder. But each person still decides for themselves which questions belong to conscience, so the core problem remains unsolved.

Check Your Understanding

1. Is Thoreau an anarchist? Answer using the text.

In the ideal, close to it (¶1: “That government is best which governs not at all”). In practice, no: ¶3 says “I ask for, not at once no government, but at once a better government,” and he separates himself from the “no-government men.”

2. What method does Thoreau use to argue that the majority cannot decide justice?

Elimination. He lists three possible reasons the majority rules (most likely right, fairest, strongest), rules out the first two, and is left with “strongest” — and strength has nothing to do with justice.

3. Why are people who serve the State “with their heads” also a problem?

Because they “rarely make any moral distinctions,” they may serve the devil without meaning to. Skill is not the same as moral judgment.
